The goal of this project was to align the color negatives to effectively clolorize the Prokudin-Gorskii photo collection. We explored doing this on a single-scale and then utilizing multi-scale to make it faster

The Method

Alignment Metrics
L2 · Euclidean Distance D(u,v) = √ Σx,y  ( I1(x,y) − I2(x+u, y+v) )2
Normalized Cross-Correlation NCC = ( I1 − Ī1 ) · ( I2 − Ī2 ) ‖ I1 − Ī1 ‖  ‖ I2 − Ī2 ‖

How it works

For both single-scale and multi-scale I chose to remove 5% off of each edge (to account for borders, etc.), which seemed to work reasonably well. I also normalized each image (defined as centering it and then dividing by the Frobenius norm). This was to account for varying intensities, but as we see it was still ineffective for the Emir.

For single scale (the following image) I did a search across displacements from (-20, 20) for both dx and dy. Then I would shift the image. Instead of using roll (I did so originally, but as you can see in the scratch files in my code I quickly abandoned that approach) I decided to use splicing. Intuitively, to me, if I stack 2 images on top of each other and I shift the top one left 15, then its left 15 pixels go off the edge (out of bounds), but the bottom bounds' right 15 also interact with nothing (out of bounds). So I splice those and then use both L2 and NCC to calculate. The downside to this approach is that big shifts compare fewer pixels, which could affect our interpretation of the loss; however, since we use a small shift of around 20 pixels, that effect doesn't really come into play. Furthermore, also, instead, by using rolling you introduce "adversarial pixels", i.e. it doesn't really make sense to compare the wrap-around, and I found that worked less.

The Image Pyramid

¼ scale
½ scale
full resolution  ·  fine
Coarse → Fine  |  estimate doubles & refines each level

The Coarse-to-Fine Idea

As mentioned above, we do the same preprocessing here (crop the border by cropping 5% off of each side, then we normalize to try to reduce the effect of intensities differing too much in magnitude). Now starts the pyramid-ing. I'll get into the actual speed reasoning, but empirically it makes sense by looking at the difference of the low-res images between single scale and multi-scale. How this works is we divide by 2 until one of our dimensions is ≤ 200 pixels. I assume this is good enough to reduce to single scale. I then run single scale on it to get an initial displacement estimate. Then I travel "up" the pyramid. Every time I travel up I multiply the previous displacement found in the previous layer by 2 to account for the scale getting bigger as I travel back up, then I do a small search of ±2 pixels to account for any new info that comes about by the better resolutions. We continue this till we get to the top level. Now, why is this faster? Well, if we think about it, one pixel in an 8× downsampled image corresponds to an 8 by 8 in the bigger image. And then you do a smaller search, but effectively, by doing the most exhaustive search at the bottom level, we are able to cover most of the range efficiently, and then each level up we do some tweaks, as opposed to doing an exhaustive ±20 on each search in the largest window. (It's the same amount of ops for single scale on high and low res, but calculating the loss etc. is faster when the image is smaller, intuitively. In multi scale we do that, and then we do small amount of op with bigger and bigger images.)

Why the raw-pixel metrics fail here

The reason the Emir fails is because the blue and gold robe looks very light on the blue pane but very dark on the red pane; in fact, the negatives almost look like inverses on the robe. Because of this, regardless of normalization, the L2 and NCC metrics find it hard to place these on top of each other because they differ by so much. This is what leads to the off shift.
